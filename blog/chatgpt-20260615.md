The Week I Started Measuring Everything

From local AI experiments to token economics, this week was all about understanding what really happens under the hood.

Over the past week I noticed a recurring pattern across almost every project I touched: I wasn’t building new features as much as I was trying to measure, understand, and control systems that normally behave like black boxes.

Local AI: Moving Beyond the API

One of the most interesting threads this week was continuing my exploration of running large language models locally.

I spent time evaluating what can realistically run on consumer hardware, particularly on an RTX 3060 with 12 GB of VRAM. Models like Gemma and Qwen continue to be surprisingly capable, but the bigger lesson wasn’t model quality—it was infrastructure.

Running a model locally is easy.

Building an environment around it that can actually read files, execute tasks, manage context, and behave like a coding assistant is where the real engineering starts.

That led me deeper into the ecosystem around Ollama, OpenCode, Hermes Agent, and the growing collection of tools trying to bridge the gap between “a model” and “an autonomous assistant”.

The Hidden Cost of AI

Another topic that kept resurfacing was tokenization.

At first glance, tokenizers seem like a boring implementation detail. But once you start building tools around AI usage, they become impossible to ignore.

I spent time investigating OpenAI’s o200k_base tokenizer and comparing it with the various tokenization strategies used by different providers.

The more I looked into it, the more obvious it became that token counting is still one of the least standardized parts of the AI ecosystem.

Every provider has its own tokenizer.

Every tokenizer produces different counts.

Every count affects cost estimation.

For projects like TokenMeter, understanding those differences isn’t optional—it is the product.

Building Better Tooling

Several conversations revolved around how developer tools could become more transparent.

Whether it’s estimating AI costs before generating code, exposing quota usage, understanding token consumption, or creating local dashboards for AI activity, I keep coming back to the same question:

“How can we make AI systems observable?”

Traditional software engineering has monitoring, metrics, logs, traces, and dashboards.

AI tooling is still in its early days by comparison.

The opportunity isn’t just building better models.

It’s building better instrumentation around those models.

Infrastructure Reality Checks

Not everything this week involved AI.

I also spent time debugging real-world infrastructure issues:

* WiFi slowdowns affecting an entire home network.
* Router saturation and traffic analysis problems.
* Audio subsystem failures on a Mac Mini.
* General performance investigations where symptoms and root causes were very different things.

These are the kinds of engineering problems that never appear in conference talks but consume a surprising amount of time in real life.

The lesson remains unchanged:

The system that fails is rarely the system you initially suspect.

Personal Branding and Career Positioning

I also revisited my CV and professional profile.

The challenge wasn’t updating technologies.

It was deciding how much emphasis to place on the growing amount of AI experimentation happening alongside my backend engineering work.

Five years ago, a backend CV focused on Java, Spring Boot, databases, and microservices.

Today, local AI infrastructure, agent orchestration, observability, and AI-assisted development are becoming increasingly relevant parts of the story.

The industry is changing quickly, and it’s becoming clear that understanding AI systems is no longer a separate skill set—it is gradually becoming part of modern software engineering.

What I’m Taking Into Next Week

The biggest takeaway from this week is surprisingly simple:

I’m becoming less interested in consuming AI products and more interested in understanding how they work.

The models are impressive.

But the surrounding ecosystem—tokenization, observability, orchestration, cost tracking, memory, tooling, and infrastructure—is where most of the engineering challenges still live.

And those challenges are exactly the kind of problems I enjoy solving.

Sometimes the most interesting project isn’t the AI itself.

It’s everything required to make the AI useful.
